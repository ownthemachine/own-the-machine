# My additional review: making the ownership mechanism executable

6 September 2026. Author: the primary coding assistant, responding to the initiator's request for its own additional insights. Law baseline `0aeaada`; site baseline `35e057c`. This is my direct reading and scenario analysis, informed by but distinct from the Fable/Gemini review of the earlier five findings. It is not a third independent blind review: I have read both outputs. No operative article, simulator or public campaign text is amended here.

My assessment: the project has a clear purpose, but legal ownership, portfolio wealth, spendable income and operational delivery are different milestones. Automation helps deliver them only after the rules specify the inputs, responsible actors and treatment of exceptions. The following six findings go beyond the earlier five; each includes a defence so a plausible concern is not presented as a proven failure.

## S1. A large automation economy does not guarantee a useful cash dividend

**Priority: high. Classification: confirmed limitation of the design and explanatory overstatement; policy choice, not proof the ownership proposal fails.**

Sources: Articles 8(4), 9(1)(d)–(e), 10(6); Annex II points 2 and 5; objection 15; site `src/scripts/simulator.ts`, particularly `run()`; site `simulator/SPEC.md`.

Article 8(4) forbids borrowing or disposing of holdings for the purpose of funding a distribution. Annex II permits realised income, not unrealised appreciation, to fund the distributable amount. Article 10(6)'s three-positive-year backstop does not manufacture income in a zero-income year.

**Counterexample:** suppose covered businesses grow greatly in value while retaining profits, and the Reserve receives shares but no dividends, interest or other eligible realised returns. Its capital can be valuable while its distributable amount is zero. This is a logical scenario, not a forecast about named companies. It contradicts objection 15's assertion that a small dividend can occur only where the underlying problem is small.

**Strongest defence:** Article 9 allows prudent diversification of crystallised holdings, so the Reserve need not hold only non-dividend-paying original shares forever. It could diversify into assets producing income. Nothing here proves it cannot ever pay or that capital appreciation has no benefit. But diversification needs market access, liquidity, a mandate and time; its permission alone does not establish a payout level or timing. It also requires a clear distinction from disposals whose purpose is funding a distribution.

**Simulator implication:** its return parameter feeds both capital growth and the distribution ceiling through `capital * r`; it does not separate cash yield from unrealised appreciation. Its header now honestly describes important omissions. The older SPEC still claims exact Annex II implementation and gives obsolete liquidity-only language. That specification should be corrected; the existing model should be treated as an illustration, not evidence this counterexample cannot happen. The band is generated by chosen input changes, not a calibrated statistical distribution, notwithstanding the SPEC's p25/p75 description.

**Direction:** show separate realised yield and appreciation assumptions; test high asset growth with low cash yield, no dividends, illiquid holdings, losses and administrative costs. Explain ownership accumulation separately from income available during job displacement. If an immediate income guarantee is intended, the present draft does not establish one; choosing another funding mechanism would be a policy decision, not a marketing correction.

## S2. The first liquidity event can occur before the warrant is issued

**Priority: high. Classification: specific timing ambiguity/gap in the general pathway.**

Sources: Articles 3(7), 5(1)–(3), 5(5), 17(2).

**Scenario:** designation on day 0; an unlisted undertaking completes its first listing on day 30; it issues its warrant on day 80, within the permitted three months. Article 5(2) names the first liquidity event **following issuance**. Article 3(7) requires designation before the event, which this sequence satisfies. The seven-year clock also starts at issuance. On a literal reading, that listing is not the trigger for the later-issued warrant.

**Strongest defence:** the statutory subscription right in Article 5(5), read purposively with Article 3(7), might be interpreted to attach at the listing anyway. Article 17(2) expressly uses events after designation for undertakings listed before entry into force, which further supports intended early coverage. But this special route is not a clear general override of Article 5(2)'s issuance condition. I do not conclude that the first event definitively escapes every judicial interpretation.

**Direction:** choose a single operative attachment date and say what happens when an event precedes physical issuance. Possible approaches include accelerated issuance before an event or a statutory right effective from designation, with administrative issuance later. Preserve due process and prospectivity. Do not rely on a fine for late issuance to fix this scenario: issuance on day 80 is timely under the present paragraph 1.

## S3. A designated economic group needs an identified legal issuer

**Priority: high. Classification: implementation ambiguity with substantive consequences.**

Sources: Article 2(1), Article 5(2), (4)(b), (5), (11); Annex I points 1, 3 and 5.

Article 2 includes linked enterprises in the undertaking and the counting method consolidates the group. A group of companies is not necessarily one legal issuer with one share capital. Article 5 then requires shares in the covered undertaking, refers to its fully diluted capital and applies class-ranking protections.

**Scenario:** a parent holds several operating subsidiaries, one with outside minority shareholders. Which entity issues the shares? Does valuation use parent equity alone, each issuer separately or consolidated group value? Treating every subsidiary's full capital as an additional base could count value already represented in the parent; treating only an operating subsidiary as the issuer could miss the designated group's economics. These are questions, not a claim that double counting is required by the draft.

**Strongest defence:** a designation decision can identify the ultimate parent and an independent valuer can determine a coherent group valuation. Annex I already asks for every linked enterprise's identity. But the text does not expressly assign issuer selection or state how the share entitlement tracks that selection, and valuation alone should not silently choose a materially different property interest. Article 5(11)'s transfer rules solve a different problem and do not fully specify initial group issuance.

**Direction:** distinguish the economic unit used for designation from the legal issuer(s) and their obligations. Specify parent/minority treatment and an aggregate cap that can be calculated without counting the same economic interest twice. Validate a simple parent, a group with minority-owned subsidiaries, a third-country parent, and an undertaking lacking ordinary share capital. This does not assert that every such structure is presently outside the law.

## S4. Universal entitlement needs a common payment-state protocol

**Priority: high for operation. Classification: implementation and rights questions, not a finding that fraud or exclusion is inevitable.**

Sources: Article 10(1)–(4), (6); Article 11(1)–(5).

The law grants an entitlement independently of registration, allows multiple national vehicles, and provides a cross-border fallback. It does not itself describe the common record date, unique registration coordination, transfer between vehicles, correction of identity errors or treatment of late registration. These affect who is paid for a distribution period, not merely user-interface design.

**Scenarios:** a dual citizen registers in two vehicles; a holder moves countries while a payment is pending; someone turns 18 halfway through a period; an eligible holder dies before a deferred distribution is declared; a citizen lives outside the EU; an eligible person registers after earlier distributions. Article 10(4)(a) refers to periods during which the holder was living and eligible, but the precise period, partial-period treatment and reconciliation process are not defined. Article 11(4)'s mandatory fallback expressly covers residence in a Member State. Article 11(2)(d)'s general access restriction supplies a strong argument against excluding citizens abroad, but the assigned processing and cost route is still unclear.

**Strongest defence:** national vehicles already operate identity and payment systems; equality and the single personal entitlement rule constrain their choices. Operational agreements and national implementation can supply details. The concern is that independent national choices about historical eligibility, duplicate payments and distribution periods could produce different outcomes for the same Union entitlement. Not every technical field belongs in the regulation.

**Direction:** specify the common rights rules and authority for interoperable implementation. Use privacy-preserving duplicate detection, idempotent payment identifiers, auditable transfers and a correction/appeal route. Do not silently add means tests, residence conditions or a new central database. Deterministic software can handle routine eligibility calculations; contested identity and entitlement need accountable decisions.

## S5. Distribution arithmetic needs an explicit order of operations

**Priority: high before relying on financial projections. Classification: accounting specification gaps requiring worked cases.**

Sources: Articles 8(4), 9(1)(e), 10(6); Annex II points 1–3.

Three concrete boundary cases expose missing precision:

1. **Costs exceed the residual income.** Let realised income be 1, retention 0.5 and administration costs 1, in arbitrary monetary units. Point 2's subtraction gives -0.5, but its express zero floor is triggered when retention alone equals or exceeds income, which is false here. A negative payment is not a reasonable intended result. Specify the floor and treatment of unpaid costs instead of leaving the exception incomplete.
2. **Subscription debt competes for the first income.** Article 9 requires subscription borrowing to be repaid from the first realised income. Annex II deducts retention and administrative costs but does not expressly sequence debt repayment in calculating distributable cash. Debt repayment changes cash and liabilities, not necessarily income. Specify the waterfall so the same cash is not counted as available for both debt and distributions. This is distinct from the already-declined claim that subscription borrowing is prohibited.
3. **Fresh contributions, capital losses and deferred payouts interact.** Point 1 compares year-end capital with the preceding year and adjusts for inflation and smoothed valuation changes; it does not explicitly segregate fresh warrant-derived capital from investment performance. Article 10(6) folds a deferred distributable amount into capital while promising it in the next distribution. Test that new contributions do not unintentionally mask losses on the old pool and that deferred distributions are tracked without being lost or charged retention twice. These risks require worked accounting examples; I do not assert a single inevitable calculation from the present wording.

**Strongest defence:** sound accounting, a non-negative meaning of “amount” and the preservation purpose may supply sensible results; administrative methodology can supply some detail. That does not make the current simulator an executable statement of these rules, and different plausible implementations must not silently determine citizens' entitlements.

**Direction:** write a normative annual state transition with named balances: opening protected capital, fresh contributed capital, realised cash income, valuation changes, debt service, costs, retention shortfall, deferred distributions and closing capital. Validate both asset-value and cash conservation. This is a specification task before a larger model is built, not an argument for more campaign employees.

## S6. The start-up calendar activates notifications before designation machinery

**Priority: medium/high. Classification: sequencing ambiguity needing clarification.**

Sources: Articles 3(3)–(4), 4(4), 8(1), 11(1), 17(1)–(2), 18(2).

Article 18 applies Article 3(3), Article 11(1) and Article 17(1) immediately at entry into force; most other provisions apply 18 months later. Initial threshold notifications are due within two months, while Article 3(4)'s 45-working-day designation machinery and Article 8's establishment of the Reserve are not expressly among the early exceptions.

**Strongest defence:** an advance notification period may be intentional preparation, and the 45-day duty might be understood to become operative only at general application. The draft does not need to collect shares on day one. But the start point of that deadline for already-complete notifications, preparation powers and treatment of liquidity events during the interval need to be clear. The drafting note says the delay gives the machinery time to exist without expressly activating all the machinery needed to prepare it.

**Direction:** publish one implementation calendar and align the articles to it: information intake, hearing, designation decisions, Reserve establishment, valuer appointments, warrant attachment and national vehicle readiness. Distinguish advance administration from substantive company obligations. Test a notification in month 2, listing in month 10 and general application in month 18 without assuming retroactive capture.

## What I would prioritise

First, resolve when the right attaches and which legal entity owes the shares (S2/S3), together with the previously identified Article 5/13 repairs. Second, agree an executable cash/distribution specification (S1/S5). Third, define accountable Reserve administration and the cross-border payment protocol (earlier governance finding plus S4), with an aligned start-up calendar (S6).

For the campaign, keep the promise tied to what the draft establishes: a durable shared claim on capital with conditional distributions. Concern about jobs is a reason to explain the timing honestly; it is not evidence that this instrument already replaces lost wages. Your engineering contribution can be a small, inspectable reference implementation that reproduces these scenarios and makes disputed choices visible. Routine work can be automated once those choices are explicit.

These are review recommendations, not adopted policy. Every operative amendment requires lint and all six repository gates; memorandum/specification corrections need the applicable prose or technical checks. Existing Treaty limits on delegated and implementing powers are relevant to deciding what belongs in the law versus later administration: [Article 290](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:12012E290) and [Article 291](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:12012E291). This review does not settle legal basis or Charter compatibility.

I retain these qualifications to the verifier's proposed solutions:

- **S2:** do not equate attachment of a contingent claim at designation with immediate vesting of the exercisable subscription. The latter would change the trigger policy. My recommendation is to decide the attachment rule explicitly, not adopt the reviewer's sentence verbatim.
- **S3/S4:** naming an addressee in a designation decision is a plausible administrative solution, already acknowledged above. It does not by itself resolve which equity bears dilution. Article 16 is committee procedure for existing conferred tasks, not a free-standing authority to legislate issuer or citizen-payment rules. The assertion that all S4 questions can safely be deferred is not adopted: technical deduplication and choices affecting period-based entitlements need different treatment. The report does not deny statutory vesting.
- **S5:** the verifier's proposed costs-before-debt-before-retention sequence is an unapproved policy/accounting choice. The present text prioritises repayment from first realised income, and any repair must reconcile that explicitly. Whether a change is within a future delegated power depends on its substance; pre-filing amendments are reviewed as substantive changes now. The cash/valuation risks remain qualified scenarios, not proved outcomes in every accounting implementation.
- **S6:** activating a broad list of articles early is one proposal, not an established minimal fix. Test the resulting rights, powers and deadlines together before choosing it. No retroactive obligation or immediate designation policy has been adopted here.
- The verifier calls the disposal restriction a ban on “forced” disposals in places; the draft's test concerns the purpose of financing a distribution, whether a sale is forced or voluntary. The report uses the latter test. Unverified case-law and general legislative-practice claims in the model output are not incorporated as authorities.
