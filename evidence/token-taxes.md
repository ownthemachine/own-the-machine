# Token and compute taxes: the competing family, and what to take from it

Asked whether taxing tokens gives a different order of magnitude and a
different approach from the warrant. The short answer is that it gives money
very much sooner, roughly comparable money in the mature state, solves one
problem this instrument has badly, and fails two of this project's four tests
outright. The two design ideas inside it are worth taking.

## What actually exists, as of August 2026

This is no longer a thought experiment. The policy space moved while this
file was being drafted.

- **H.R. 10044, the AI Tax and Work Protection Act**, introduced 6 August
  2026 by Greg Casar and two colleagues: a levy on AI companies' token sales
  and product revenue, **with rates that escalate automatically when national
  unemployment rises above five per cent.**
- **Mark Cuban's proposal**, August 2026: under fifty cents per million
  tokens, levied at the provider, estimated at about USD 10 billion a year at
  the outset and rising sharply with inference demand.
- **FLOP or compute taxes**: levied on the volume of compute used to train or
  run systems, discussed by several firms and in the trade press.
- **A position paper**, arXiv 2603.04555, arguing token taxes on
  enforceability grounds: they run through existing compute-governance
  infrastructure, and they are levied **at the point of use rather than the
  point of hosting**.

## Order of magnitude

Cuban's figure implies inference volume of the order of tens of quadrillions
of tokens a year, which is consistent with published platform disclosures. On
a Union share of global inference of roughly a fifth, a levy at his rate
raises single-digit billions of euros a year today, which is of the order of
**EUR 6 to 8 per adult per year, immediately**.

That is not large. What matters is the shape: it is a flow that begins at
once and grows with usage, where the warrant is a stock that crystallises
rarely and pays nothing for years. On this file's own model the Reserve pays
about EUR 3 per adult per year at year forty on the currently designated set,
and EUR 278 to 444 only if designation comes to reach a quarter to two fifths
of world equity. A token levy reaches EUR 6 to 8 in year one.

In a mature displacement scenario the two converge. If systems perform work
equivalent to a fifth of Union labour compensation and inference is billed at
something like a tenth of the value delivered, a levy in the range of ten to
thirty per cent of token spend yields of the order of EUR 90 to 275 per adult
per year, against EUR 278 to 444 for the warrant at three per cent in the
same world. **So the honest comparison is not an order of magnitude in the
steady state. It is one to two orders of magnitude in the first two decades,
which is exactly the period this instrument is weakest.**

## Why this file does not adopt it

A token levy fails two of the four tests in GOVERNANCE.md on its face.

**Test 1, assets not flows.** A consumption levy on inference is a flow. The
citizen receives a transfer, not a holding. That is objection 13, universal
basic income with extra steps, and this file's entire thesis is that the
difference between a benefit and a stake is the difference between something
renegotiated annually and something owned.

**Test 3, raid-proof.** A tax is repealable by the majority that enacted it.
Objection 9 is about exactly this, and the evidence base carries Poland,
Hungary, Ireland and the Swedish wage-earner funds as precedents for what
happens to accumulated public money when a government wants it. A
shareholding is property; a levy is a line in a budget.

And in Union law it is worse, not better. A token tax is unambiguously a
fiscal measure: Article 113 TFEU, unanimity, twenty-seven vetoes. The entire
architecture of the warrant exists to stay outside that, which is objection
2. Being economically larger does not help if the legal route is closed. The
United States can pass H.R. 10044 by simple majorities; the Union cannot pass
its equivalent at all without Ireland and Luxembourg agreeing.

## What to take from it

Two ideas, both directly portable, and the second may be the most useful
thing this research produced.

**1. Point of use rather than point of hosting.** The strongest argument in
the token-tax literature is that a usage-based charge is levied where the
service is consumed, which removes the extraterritoriality problem entirely.
This file has that problem acutely: evidence/designation-count.md shows the
designated set is currently two United States undertakings, and hostile
counsel calls the instrument extraterritorial asset seizure. The warrant
cannot become a usage charge, but the **designation and valuation basis can
be anchored to Union usage** rather than to global entity value, which
answers the same objection with the same logic while staying asset-based.
That is a drafting question for Article 3 and Annex I, and it should be taken
up with the threshold work.

**2. The Casar escalator.** Tying the rate to measured unemployment is the
conditional design this instrument has been missing. Objection 21 records
that Article 1's objective is qualitative, so the necessity limb of Article
52(1) has nothing to test against, and evidence/sizing-the-ask.md shows the
instrument is only meaningful in the world where displacement is large. Both
problems have the same answer: **make the warrant percentage a function of
measured displacement rather than a fixed number.**

That would mean the instrument takes little or nothing in a world where
automation does not displace labour, which is the world where it has no
justification anyway, and takes more as displacement rises, which is the
world it was written for. It makes the objective quantified and testable, it
makes necessity arguable from a measurement rather than from an assertion,
and it improves the Article 17 position rather than worsening it, because a
percentage that tracks a measured harm is proportionate by construction in a
way a flat three per cent is not.

It also has costs that must be stated: an escalator introduces the
discretionary, politically contestable element that DC-3 was written to keep
out, and a designation regime whose intensity moves with a published
statistic invites gaming of that statistic. Whether those costs are bearable
is the question for the next drafting round, and it should go through the
full pipeline rather than be adopted because it is elegant.

## What this means for the campaign

The competing family is now legislative rather than academic, in the United
States, with a bill number and a date. That is worth knowing before the
September soundings, for two reasons. It shows the problem is being taken
seriously by people with drafting power, which helps. And it means anyone
this file approaches may already have a token tax in mind as the obvious
answer, so the memorandum needs to be able to say in two sentences why this
instrument is not that one, and why the reason is legal and durable rather
than merely different.
