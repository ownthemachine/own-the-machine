# Europe does not replicate the American picture

Research note, 18 August 2026. Strand 1 of 3 of the European evidence base.
Commissioned to find European replacements for the American numbers carrying
the book's argument. It found something more useful and less comfortable.

## THE FINDING THAT CHANGES THE POST

**Do not argue in Europe that labour's share is collapsing. It is not, and the
data is one download away from anyone who wants to check.**

AMECO adjusted wage share, % GDP at factor cost (verified at source, release
3 June 2026):

| | 1995 | 2007 | 2025 | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **EU27** | 64.5 | 61.0 | **63.3** | fell 1.2 points in thirty years |
| Euro area 20 | 64.9 | 61.2 | 63.9 | |
| **Germany** | 65.3 | 60.0 | **66.1** | **series high, in 2025** |
| **France** | 66.1 | 63.5 | **67.3** | above its 1995 level |
| Belgium | 69.0 | 65.8 | 64.5 | peak was 72.7 in 1981 |
| Netherlands | 67.7 | 61.9 | 62.8 | peak was 75.7 in 1975 |
| Poland | 65.1 | 55.6 | 59.7 | the real European illustration |

The 2022 profit spike also **fully reversed**: euro-area non-financial corporate
gross profit share peaked at 42.3% in 2021-Q2 and is 38.6% in 2026-Q1, *below*
its 2019 level of 39.7% (Eurostat nasq_10_ki, updated 18 Aug 2026, verified).

**Belgium is the exception and is therefore the better national card:** its
corporate profit share went 35.4% (1999) to 45.3% (2022, all-time high) to 44.7%
(2026-Q1). Belgium never gave the increase back while the euro area gave all of
it back. But note the NBB finds Belgian unit profits contributed *negatively* to
2023 inflation, because indexation pushed unit labour costs up first.

## WHAT TO ARGUE INSTEAD, AND IT IS STRONGER

Not "labour's share is falling" but **"almost nobody in Europe owns the capital
side at all."** That claim is overwhelming, current, and from the ECB's own
flagship survey.

**ECB Household Finance and Consumption Survey, wave 5 (2023 reference year),
published June 2026, % of households** (verified at source):

| | Owns main residence | **Owns listed shares** |
|---|---|---|
| **Euro area** | **60.1** | **11.4** |
| Belgium | 66.7 | 12.6 |
| Netherlands | 56.6 | 6.3 |
| Italy | 74.5 | **4.4** |
| **Greece** | 68.2 | **1.1** |
| Finland | 64.0 | 25.0 |

**ECB Distributional Wealth Accounts, 2025-Q4** (verified, recomputed from EUR
levels because no percentage series exists): the top 10% of euro-area households
hold **82.6%** of directly held listed shares; the bottom 50% hold **2.2%**.
Italy 93.3% / 0.5%.

**Portfolio composition, euro area, bottom 50% of households:** 62.9% housing,
25.2% deposits, **3.4% equity and funds, 0.7% listed shares**. Belgium's bottom
half: **76.4% housing, 2.1% equity.** That is the book's thesis as a single row.

## THE SLEEPER NUMBER

**Belgian households have accrued EUR 1,740bn of pension claims and own EUR 136bn
of it. About 92% is a promise from future taxpayers, not an asset on anyone's
balance sheet.** OECD/Eurostat Table 29, accrued-to-date pension entitlements,
2021, verified from the OECD SDMX API. Netherlands 49.1% funded, Sweden 33.1%,
Germany 7.7%, **France 0.0%**.

Companion: OECD asset-backed pension assets as % of GDP, 2024 (verified):
Denmark 206.4, Netherlands 150.9, USA 153.3, Sweden 115.8, Belgium 30.3,
France 12.9, **Germany 6.4**. Caveat to state yourself: Germany's excludes
Direktzusage book reserves and so understates it.

## CORRECTIONS TO WHAT WE ALREADY HELD

- **Do not print the Dutch 69.9%.** CBS revised it. Current vintage (8 July
  2026): market-sector AIQ **81.4% in 1995 to 70.6% in 2025**, a fall of 10.8
  points. Two further warnings: it has now risen three years running, so the
  fall is a 1995-2022 story rather than a live one; and the "revisie 2021"
  benchmark revision cut recent years about 3.9 points while leaving 1995
  untouched, roughly doubling the measured decline. Anyone quoting a pre-2024
  source will get a different number.
- **The US comparison cuts against the obvious framing.** 58.0% of US families
  hold equity directly or indirectly (Fed SCF 2022) against 11.4% of euro-area
  households holding listed shares, and the US top decile holds 81.7% of equity
  against the euro area's 85.7%. American ownership is BROADER, almost entirely
  through the retirement-account system. That is awkward for any "Anglo-Saxon
  capitalism concentrates more" line, and it is the single best evidence that
  the broadening-ownership remedy can actually work at population scale.

## TRAPS

1. **Three defensible labour-share measures differ by up to 19 points.** Italy
   2024: unadjusted 39.4%, adjusted 58.3%. Never quote one without naming it.
   Karabarbounis-Neiman used the corporate measure, so if the book keeps that
   citation the European comparators must be corporate too.
2. **Do not use Norway's wage share.** Its 37.3% in 2022 is the gas-price spike
   inflating the denominator, not a labour-market event.
3. **Germany has a hard break at 1991.** Never splice West Germany to unified
   Germany. EU27 and euro-area aggregates only exist from 1995.
4. **Every major European household-wealth statistic excludes pension
   entitlements**, which inverts the ranking: the Netherlands and Denmark, whose
   households genuinely do own productive claims, appear housing-dominated and
   asset-poor.
5. **WID, HFCS and DWA figures must never share a table.** Different units
   (per-adult versus household) and methods. The Dutch triangulation shows the
   spread: HFCS 44.8%, DWA 52.0%, CBS tax register 56.1% for the same top-10%
   share.
6. **The ILO's 14.2-point productivity-wage gap is unverified** and the ILO
   itself says most of the gap opened before 2006. Verify before printing.
7. **ETUI contradicts itself across editions**: its 2019 report shows wages
   lagging productivity in 15 of 28 countries; its 2026 edition says real wages
   grew faster than productivity in 2021-2025 in every member state bar two.
8. **The OECD decomposition is the thing a hostile reader will bring.** For
   ~1995-2013 it finds median wages grew FASTER than productivity in France,
   Italy, Spain, Finland, Sweden, Denmark and the UK. Belgium's total decoupling
   is only -0.4pp a year, the Netherlands -0.7pp, against the US -1.3pp.

## THE FIVE STRONGEST NUMBERS, RANKED BY HOW HARD THEY ARE TO DISMISS

1. **In the euro area, 60% of households own their home and 11% own shares in a
   company.** Belgium 67 versus 13. Italy 75 versus 4. Greece 68 versus 1.
   ECB HFCS wave 5, published June 2026. Same file, same population, same survey.
2. **The richest tenth of euro-area households own 83% of directly held shares;
   the poorest half own 2%.** ECB DWA 2025-Q4. Caveat: every DWA observation is
   flagged as an ECB estimate.
3. **The typical Dutch household is worth EUR 135,500, or EUR 23,000 once you
   take away the house.** CBS 83834NED, 1 Jan 2024, from tax registers rather
   than a survey. State the pension exclusion yourself before a critic does.
4. **Belgian households have built up EUR 1,740bn of pension claims and own
   EUR 136bn of it.** OECD/Eurostat Table 29. A critic can only reply that a
   state promise is as good as an asset, which is the argument the book wants.
5. **Danish households hold pension assets worth 206% of national output.
   German households hold 6%.** OECD, 2024. Softest of the five because of the
   German book-reserve exclusion.
